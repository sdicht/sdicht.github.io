# EXERCISE — Hawk Roosting: Poetic Voice (pairs)

Read "Hawk Roosting" carefully and answer the following questions:

1. Wolosky argues that in a dramatic monologue there are four participants: speaker, addressee, poet, and reader. Identify the speaker in this poem. Is there an addressee? If not, what is the effect of the hawk speaking to no one in particular — or to everyone?

2. Wolosky says that a dramatic monologue exploits the gap between what the speaker intends to reveal and what the reader actually understands. What does the hawk think it is saying about itself? What does the reader understand that the hawk does not? Point to specific lines.

3. You have already analyzed how syntax constructs meaning in poetry. Look at the dominant sentence structure in "Hawk Roosting" — its length, its tense, its paratactic or hypotactic character. How does the syntax perform the hawk's personality? Compare briefly to "Prufrock"'s syntax or "My Last Duchess" — what does the contrast tell you about the difference between these constructed voices?

4. If the hawk is the speaker and not Hughes, where is Hughes in the poem? What does Hughes communicate through the hawk that he could not say directly as a lyric I?
